PingHub

A trip-planning app for friend groups, designed and usability-tested end-to-end on the User side — testing validated that group-availability visualization and built-in voting were the two features that actually solved the "we can never agree" problem.

Tag 01 — The Problem

Nobody can agree — so nobody books.

Friend groups who want to travel together run into the same wall every time: schedules don't line up, nobody can agree on a destination, and the planning gets scattered across five different tools — a group chat for logistics, Instagram for destination ideas, separate review sites, a shared doc for the itinerary. The result is trips that get delayed, downsized, or quietly abandoned before anyone books anything.

Our team set out to design PingHub: a single platform where a group can invite friends, vote on free dates, agree on a destination, and build a shared itinerary together — solving the coordination problem, not just the itinerary problem.

Context & constraints: a one-term university project built by a four-person team, split across the User side (friend groups planning a trip) and the Owner side (local businesses promoting routes and deals). I owned the User side end-to-end. Everything was built and tested in Figma with no dev handoff — every decision had to hold up on its own, without an engineer catching gaps downstream.

Tag 02 — Understanding the User

Two personas, one shared tension.

To ground the design in real behavior, the team built two personas representing opposite ends of the "friend group trip" spectrum.

Wa

22 · University student · Group of 3

Plans quickly, and pulls destination ideas from YouTube, TikTok, and Google — favoring adventure activities over choice of accommodation.

Pain points: coordinating everyone's free time is hard, and her preferences often clash with the group's.

Kong

28 · Office worker · Group of 6

Plans roughly two weeks out, and cares more about budget and variety of activities.

Pain points: consolidating input from many people is difficult, and the group struggles to actually settle on a decision.

Both point to the same tension PingHub is built to resolve: small groups need quick consensus tools; larger groups need structure for reconciling many opinions.

Journey Mapping

As a team, we mapped the emotional journey of a friend trying to organize a trip — before, during, and after using PingHub.

Before: excitement at seeing a travel photo on social media quickly turns into frustration once the group tries to lock down a date and destination — the frustration that sends the user looking for a dedicated tool. On service: the emotional dip shifts to the moment of group decision-making — waiting on votes, reconciling free days — but recovers as the itinerary comes together. After: rating the trip, writing reviews, and reusing the itinerary for a future trip reinforce a positive "I'd use this again" note — also the moment users are most likely to recommend PingHub to another group.

This before/during/after arc directly shaped what we prioritized: reducing friction at the scheduling and voting stages, since that's where the emotional dip is steepest.

From Insights to Structure: User Story Mapping

We translated the journey into a User Story Map covering the full flow: log in → create trip → invite friends → select free days → the trip owner locks in a date from the overlap → members confirm → propose and vote on places/activities → auto-estimate budget → plan the day-by-day itinerary → share the final plan.

Flagged for later: letting members shortlist preferences before the trip is even created, so the first vote has less blank-page friction. Scoped out of this build.

Tag 03 — My Role

Designing the User-side experience

I owned the front-end design for the User Role side — the 10 screens a friend group actually uses to plan a trip together.

  • Create Trip — name, dates (fixed or wait for group vote), trip type, members, destination, notes
  • Trip Detail — itinerary, destination, per-person budget, active votes, shared notes
  • Create Vote — topic, single/multiple choice, options, deadline
  • Recommended Route — suggested route, sortable by price or reviews, with a proposed schedule
  • Select Free Day — availability overlap as a calendar heatmap, best shared range surfaced automatically
  • Create Post — sharing a trip's highlights, tips, and photos
  • Map — search and preview a destination's ratings and reviews
  • Invited User's Trip Detail — same detail screen an invitee sees, with Accept/Decline
  • Voting — live counts, days remaining, option to add a choice
  • Edit Route — add, remove, or re-time itinerary activities

Full-team effort

Research, scoping, and the user journey (personas, journey map, story map) were built together as a four-person team — Sasiyakorn Chansiri (me), Nakul Chalermchaikosol, Tanagorn Fukuhara, and Pongsapak Tatongjai.

Individual ownership

My individual ownership was the User Role flow — the 10 screens above, designed and wired into an interactive prototype in Figma, with no dev handoff.

Tag 04 — Critical Decisions

Three decisions that shaped the User side.

Decision 01

Making group availability visible at a glance

Insight: both personas — Wa's tight-knit trio and Kong's larger crew — named the same top pain point: nobody can tell when everyone is actually free.

Decision: instead of asking the trip owner to manually cross-reference five calendars, Select Free Day renders everyone's availability as a heatmap — darker means more people are free — so the best shared window is obvious without doing any math.

Outcome: one of the two features usability testers specifically called out as most useful.

Decision 02

Confirming before irreversible group actions

Insight: journey mapping showed the sharpest emotional dip happens right at group decision-making — the worst outcome in a shared-decision app is someone accidentally locking the group into something nobody agreed on.

Decision: accepting or declining a trip invite triggers a lightweight confirmation modal before the action is final, applied consistently across every group-affecting action.

Outcome: the pattern testers pointed to when describing the app as easy to trust.

Decision 03

Preventing errors before they happen, not after

Insight: Create Trip is the very first thing a new user does — friction here risks losing the group before planning even starts.

Decision: required fields validate inline with plain-language prompts, and the date section uses mutually exclusive choices (fixed dates or wait for the group's vote) so the form can't be filled out in a contradictory state.

Outcome: testers moved through trip creation with zero friction points — the cleanest part of the flow in testing.

One consistency choice worth noting: voting on a destination and voting on a travel method reuse the same component — single/multiple choice, live counts, deadline — so once someone learns it once, they've learned it everywhere.

Tag 06 — Testing & Impact

What testing actually confirmed.

Since the whole premise of PingHub is "does this actually work for a group of people," we ran the prototype through its full real flow with test participants — create a trip, invite friends, select free dates, vote, build the itinerary, edit details, share the group link.

What testing caught

  • Some buttons were too small to tap comfortably
  • A few status indicators weren't clear at a glance

What it validated

  • Navigation and the core flow were validated as intuitive — testers completed trip creation, voting, and itinerary editing without getting lost
  • Availability visualization and voting — tied directly to our personas' pain points — were independently named as the most useful parts of the app
  • Two concrete UI issues were caught and fixed before final delivery
Tag 07 — Reflection

What I'd carry into the next project.

Small details compound

People tend to say the big things are fine and then quietly stumble on small execution details — a button that's just slightly too small, a status label that doesn't quite say "still pending." Those frictions rarely show up when demoing your own happy path; they only show up once someone else's thumb misses the target.

Design for the group, not the user

Designing for a group decision, not a single user's decision, meant the riskiest failure mode wasn't a typo or a broken link — it was one person accidentally locking in a choice nobody else had agreed to. Next time, I'd want to pressure-test that specific risk earlier, during wireframing, rather than catching it during usability testing.

What's next

Directions the team scoped but didn't build:

  • Push notifications for votes and trip updates
  • AI-based destination recommendations tailored to a group's interests
  • Calendar sync
  • A lightweight badge system for completed trips
